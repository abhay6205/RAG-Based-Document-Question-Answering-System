A black hole forms when a sufficiently massive star runs out of nuclear fuel and collapses under its own gravity. If the collapsing core exceeds a certain mass threshold, no known force can stop the collapse, and the material compresses into a point of infinite density called a singularity, surrounded by a boundary called the event horizon.

Once anything, including light, crosses the event horizon, it cannot escape, which is why black holes appear black and cannot be observed directly. Astronomers instead detect them indirectly, by observing their gravitational effects on nearby stars and gas, or by capturing images of the glowing material spiraling into them, as the Event Horizon Telescope did in 2019.

Supermassive black holes, with masses millions to billions of times that of the sun, are thought to exist at the center of nearly every large galaxy, including the Milky Way, where one called Sagittarius A* resides.
